When I asked Miles Bowling to introduce himself, the Mead Elementary fourth grader got right to it.
“My name is Miles, and I built an adaptive playground and I have a story.”
He could tell me exactly how long the playground took to build: five years, one month and 19 days. He remembered how nervous he was at the start, when there was so much money to raise and he had no idea whether they could do it. And when he talked about getting it done, he talked about the people who said yes.
That’s what stayed with me about Miles. He had a clear sense of what he wanted to change, and he had spent years bringing other people into the work. You could hear how proud he was of what they had built together. You could also hear how much he was already thinking about what else they could do.
Miles has cerebral palsy and uses a wheelchair. For years, the closest adaptive playground his family knew of was 45 minutes away. They wanted a place close to home where Miles could swing and play alongside his brothers and friends. They started with a lemonade stand to raise money for an accessible swing at their neighborhood park. That first fundraiser brought in $11,000.
“We can go bigger than this,” Miles recalled thinking.
His family partnered with Star Playgrounds, held two lemonade stands each summer and spoke to groups about why a playground designed for children of different abilities mattered. The effort raised $1.6 million in cash. With donated goods and services, his mom, Lauren Bowling, estimates the total value of the project at $3 million.
“I’m just a mom who asked a question, and 1,001 people said, ‘Yes, how can we help?’” Lauren said.

### Central and Niwot Elementary Earn National i-Ready Awards for Exceptional Growth

Central Elementary and Niwot Elementary have earned 2026 national i-Ready Super Stretch Schools Awards for exceptional student growth in reading. The two St. Vrain Valley schools are among just 74 schools nationwide and five in Colorado recognized in reading this year.
The awards, presented by Curriculum Associates, recognize schools where at least 55% of students meet their Stretch Growth goals, with a minimum of 200 students participating in both beginning- and end-of-year assessments.
i-Ready supports personalized learning in reading and mathematics. Its Growth Model includes two measures: Typical Growth, which reflects expected annual progress, and Stretch Growth, which sets an ambitious, individualized goal to help students reach or maintain grade-level proficiency.
St. Vrain is also recognizing Lyons Elementary and Columbine Elementary for exceptional student growth. Both schools met the same benchmark of at least 55% of students reaching their Stretch Growth goals, but their assessment participation counts fell slightly below the 200-student minimum required for the national award.

Patty Cisneros Prevo’s two Paralympic gold medals made their way from student to student at Northridge Elementary. Then the students headed outside to the basketball court, where they took turns trying out the sports wheelchairs she had brought. They were all smiles as they discovered how much skill it takes to play wheelchair basketball.
Cisneros Prevo visited Northridge the same day author Jolene Gutiérrez visited Columbine Elementary. Both visits grew out of St. Vrain Valley Schools’ partnership with the University of Colorado Boulder’s Children’s Book Festival.
At Northridge, Cisneros Prevo held students’ attention as she talked about daily life as a wheelchair user and challenged assumptions about what people who use wheelchairs can do. She shared two of her books. In
All of Apolonia
, a girl brings her cousins and teammates together for a game of wheelchair basketball on her birthday.
Tenacious
introduces readers to 15 athletes with disabilities and shares the joys and challenges they experience in their sports and daily lives.
On the court, students had fun finding out for themselves how challenging the game could be. The gold medals they’d just passed around took on new meaning once they’d had a turn in the chairs.
At Columbine, the room buzzed when Gutiérrez surprised students with their own copies of
Unbreakable: A Japanese American Family in an American Incarceration Camp
, a book she co-wrote with Minoru Tonai. She signed each copy to the student receiving it, and students were eager to open their books and look through them with friends.
Gutiérrez spoke with the whole group about the writing process and how writers keep learning and improving. She told students she spent 12 years writing before publishing her first book, sharing what it took to stick with a dream that was years in the making. Afterward, she stayed for lunch with a small group of fifth graders.
Spending time with the people behind the books gave students something to carry into the next story they read. They might pause a little longer over a character’s choices or ask a friend what they noticed on the same page. That same curiosity can shape how they listen when someone tells a story of their own.

From classroom visits and school ceremonies to musical performances and community events, students across St. Vrain Valley Schools recognize the service and sacrifices of military veterans.
Under a clear November sky, students and staff at Longs Peak Middle School gathered around the school’s flagpole Wednesday morning for a Veterans Day tradition that has connected generations of students with local military veterans.
With the entire school assembled outside, community veterans joined students for a flag-raising ceremony recognizing the service, dedication, and sacrifices of those who have served in the United States Armed Forces.
The ceremony was one of many Veterans Day observances taking place across St. Vrain Valley Schools, where students participated in activities designed to deepen their understanding of military service, American history, civic responsibility, and the importance of honoring veterans.
Throughout the district, schools marked the occasion through special breakfasts, classroom visits from veterans, student performances, and commemorative ceremonies. Students also extended their appreciation beyond school walls, with high school marching bands participating in Longmont’s annual Veterans Day Parade.
At Longs Peak Middle School, the day’s events reflected a particularly meaningful tradition of remembrance and community connection.
Principal Karin Blough joined longtime community veteran Ralph Bozella in leading the morning ceremony. Bozella, a U.S. Army veteran who served in Vietnam and has dedicated decades to supporting fellow veterans, has helped organize Veterans Day observances at Longs Peak since 1995.
As students gathered around the flagpole, the school’s choir performed the national anthem. Students then assisted visiting veterans in raising the American flag, participating directly in a ceremony that connected the school’s daily traditions with the significance of Veterans Day.
Veteran Steve Butner also addressed the students, speaking about the meaning of the American flag and the importance of Veterans Day as an opportunity to honor those who have served in the United States Armed Forces. His remarks encouraged students to reflect on the service and sacrifices of military members and their families.
The ceremony concluded with the entire school reciting the Pledge of Allegiance, a familiar part of the school day given additional significance by the presence of the veterans being honored.
Following the outdoor ceremony, veterans gathered in the school library for a special breakfast hosted in their honor. Students continued the morning’s tribute with a medley of patriotic songs performed by the school choir, creating an opportunity for veterans, educators, and members of the school community to connect.
A Lasting Legacy of Service
Among the guests were Rick and Debbie Anderson, whose annual participation carries particular significance for the Longs Peak Middle School community.
Their son, Christopher “Doc” Anderson, was a former Longs Peak student who went on to serve as a U.S. Navy hospital corpsman assigned to the Marine Corps. Anderson was killed in action in Iraq on December 4, 2006, at the age of 24.
His service and sacrifice remain part of the school’s history through a dedicated tribute within its longstanding veterans memorial mural. The display serves as a lasting reminder of the personal stories behind military service and the connections between the school, its alumni, and the broader community.
The Anderson family’s continued presence at the annual ceremony helps carry that legacy forward, allowing new generations of students to recognize that the history they learn is also connected to people and families in their own community.
For students, these personal connections offer an opportunity to develop empathy, gratitude, and an understanding of the sacrifices made by service members and their families.

Skyline High School has a few new championship banners to hang.
The Falcons finished the fall season with four Colorado League titles, including a boys golf championship that had been 25 years in the making, a pair of cross country titles, and a girls volleyball championship that ended a 16-year drought.
For Assistant Principal and Athletic Director Alan Culpepper, who joined Skyline this year, the results have been exciting. But what’s been even more encouraging is watching students and coaches begin to recognize what their programs are capable of accomplishing.
“It’s not just one thing to be competitive, but to actually win games and win matches and win races. It takes a different mindset,” Culpepper said.
That shift has come during a year of significant change for Skyline athletics. A move to the Colorado League for most sports, a new stadium, a renovated gymnasium, and new leadership have brought fresh opportunities for students and coaches alike.
A Fall Worth Celebrating
The boys golf team started things off with Skyline’s first league championship in 25 years. The Falcons dominated the league tournament, sweeping the top five individual positions. Zack Lacrosse also qualified for the state tournament, continuing his success among Colorado’s top high school golfers.
Cross country followed with two more championships, although those titles came down to much closer finishes.
Both the boys and girls teams won their respective league championships by narrow margins. On the girls side, runners who had competed at the junior varsity level during parts of the season stepped into varsity roles for the championship meet, helping secure the team title.
The races were so close that every position mattered.
For Culpepper, the experience was particularly rewarding. The school doesn’t even have an existing cross country championship banner to update. They’ll need to order a new one.
Girls volleyball added another highlight to the season, winning its first league championship in approximately 16 years and earning a trip to regionals.
The team was young, with as many as three freshmen starting at times, but continued to improve throughout the season. Head coach Kevin Ratzlaff was named the league’s Co-Coach of the Year, and freshman Naomi Victor earned Player of the Year honors.
At regionals, Skyline faced some of the state’s strongest competition, including top-ranked Lewis-Palmer. The Falcons battled through difficult matches and showed they could compete against high-level programs.
Boys soccer also made significant strides. The Falcons finished just one spot outside the state playoff field, ranking 33rd when 32 teams qualified. Although the team narrowly missed the postseason, several players earned All-League recognition after a season filled with competitive matches.
Football also showed growth this fall while competing in a challenging league. Under the leadership of head coach Justin Vogt and his staff, the Falcons continued to build their program, increasing student participation and emphasizing hard work, accountability, and teamwork. Culpepper praised the coaching staff for keeping students engaged and focused on improvement throughout the season, reinforcing that success isn’t measured by wins and losses alone.
More Than Winning
Culpepper knows a thing or two about competition.
Before moving into education and athletic leadership, he represented the United States in two Olympic Games, competed on four World Championships teams, won the U.S. Olympic Marathon Trials, and finished fourth in the Boston Marathon.
Looking back, he doesn’t focus much on the individual results. What stands out is the work it took to get there.
“It really boiled down to that pursuit of excellence more than anything else,” Culpepper said. “I was always pushing to get the most out of myself.”

### Second Graders Discover Science Close to Home at Sandstone Ranch

- Published: 2026-11-06T13:11:40-07:00
- Source URL: https://www.svvsd.org/2026/11/06/second-graders-discover-science-close-to-home-at-sandstone-ranch/

Gathered around a container of pond water, Longmont Estates Elementary second graders leaned closer. They slowed down, watching intently as they discovered the small creatures living in the water. Along the pond’s shore, a snapping turtle sunning itself inspired another quiet gathering. Students observed in awe, careful to leave it undisturbed.
These moments of discovery shaped a recent field trip to Sandstone Ranch, where students explored how the natural world connects to what they are learning in their classrooms.
“This field trip connects with second grade’s study of habitats and plant and animal adaptations,” said Kyle Houghton, a second-grade teacher at Longmont Estates Elementary.
Longmont Estates is one of 25 schools participating in the program, which is offered at no cost to schools. According to Mike O’Toole, St. Vrain Valley Schools’ science coordinator, more than 12,000 second graders have participated over the past 15 years. The experience gives students an opportunity to explore the natural resources within their own community, supported by City of Longmont volunteers who share their time and love of nature.
Students rotated through four stations, each offering a different way to investigate the landscape and the living things that depend on it.
At the water station, students examined pond life and learned how wildlife populations can provide clues about water quality. A container of pond water became an opportunity to pause, look closely, and discover life they might otherwise have overlooked.
Another station connected the landscape to the area’s geological history. Students learned about Sandstone Ranch’s rock formations while practicing their cardinal directions with the help of a memorable phrase: “Never Eat Soggy Waffles.”
Binoculars offered a closer look at another part of the habitat. Students searched for bird nests in trees and along cliffs, learning how birds build shelters and use the landscape to meet their needs.
At the barn and surrounding fields, students considered what life might have been like when the area was settled and explored ways to find food. They learned how different types of scat can help identify and track animals, swept nets through the grass to catch grasshoppers, and had the opportunity to try commercially prepared, cooked and seasoned crickets.
For many, tasting a cricket was simply a chance to try something new. A few particularly enthusiastic students enjoyed the experience enough to ask for more.
Throughout the day, volunteers helped students connect their discoveries to a larger understanding of the environment, encouraging them to observe carefully and respect the wildlife around them. O’Toole emphasized his appreciation for the volunteers and the value of helping children explore the community they call home.
For the second graders, that exploration brought classroom science into focus. Whether peering into pond water, locating a nest through binoculars, or quietly watching a turtle, students experienced the joy of discovering how much there is to learn close to home.
